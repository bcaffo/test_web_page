Regression with missing outcomes and predictors

A probabilistic perspective on \(Y\mid X\)

The central idea

Missing observations are unknown random variables.

\[p(\theta\mid D_{\mathrm{obs}})=\int p(\theta,D_{\mathrm{mis}}\mid D_{\mathrm{obs}})\,dD_{\mathrm{mis}}\]

Inference integrates over what we did not observe.

Start with a conditional model

\[Y_i\mid X_i,\theta\sim p(y_i\mid x_i,\theta)\]

What changes when both \(Y\) and \(X\) can be missing?

We generally need to move from a conditional model to a joint model.

Notation

\[Y=(Y_{\mathrm{obs}},Y_{\mathrm{mis}})\qquad X=(X_{\mathrm{obs}},X_{\mathrm{mis}})\]

Let \(R\) record the observation pattern:

\[R_{Yi}=1\ \text{if }Y_i\text{ observed},\qquad R_{Xij}=1\ \text{if }X_{ij}\text{ observed}.\]

A zero indicates missingness.

Three sets of parameters

\(\theta\)

Outcome model

\(p(Y\mid X,\theta)\)

\(\phi\)

Covariate model

\(p(X\mid\phi)\)

\(\psi\)

Missingness model

\(p(R\mid Y,X,\psi)\)

Complete-data model

\[\begin{aligned}p(Y,X,R,\theta,\phi,\psi)={}&p(R\mid Y,X,\psi)\\&\times p(Y\mid X,\theta)\\&\times p(X\mid\phi)\\&\times p(\theta,\phi,\psi).\end{aligned}\]

Selection-model factorization; prior independence is not required.

Observed-data likelihood

\[\begin{aligned}L(\theta,\phi,\psi)={}&\iint p(R\mid Y,X,\psi)\\&\quad p(Y\mid X,\theta)p(X\mid\phi)\\&\quad dY_{\mathrm{mis}}\,dX_{\mathrm{mis}}.\end{aligned}\]

Hold observed components fixed; integrate or sum over missing components.

Three missingness mechanisms

MCAR

\(p(R\mid Y,X,\psi)=p(R\mid\psi)\)

MAR

\(p(R\mid Y,X,\psi)=p(R\mid Y_{\mathrm{obs}},X_{\mathrm{obs}},\psi)\)

MNAR

Missingness still depends on unobserved values.

MAR equality is pattern-specific and must hold across possible missing values consistent with the observed data.

Ignorability

Under MAR and distinct missingness parameters / suitable prior factorization:

\[\begin{aligned}p(\theta,\phi\mid D_{\mathrm{obs}})\propto{}&p(\theta,\phi)\\&\times\iint p(Y\mid X,\theta)p(X\mid\phi)\\&\qquad dY_{\mathrm{mis}}\,dX_{\mathrm{mis}}.\end{aligned}\]

We may omit the missingness mechanism—not the missing predictors.

A Gaussian example

\[Y_i\mid X_i,\alpha,\beta,\sigma^2\sim N(\alpha+\beta X_i,\sigma^2)\]
\[X_i\mid\mu_x,\tau^2\sim N(\mu_x,\tau^2)\]

Assume independent subjects and ignorable missingness.

Missing \(Y\), observed \(X\)

\[\int p(y_i\mid x_i,\theta)\,dy_i=1\]

The missing outcome contributes no direct information about \(\theta\) in this independent-subject model.

Its observed \(X_i\) can still inform \(\phi\).

Observed \(Y\), missing \(X\)

\[p(y_i\mid\theta,\phi)=\int p(y_i\mid x_i,\theta)p(x_i\mid\phi)\,dx_i\]

A model for \(X\) is now essential to this likelihood.

The marginal outcome distribution

For the Gaussian model:

\[Y_i\sim N\!\left(\alpha+\beta\mu_x,\;\sigma^2+\beta^2\tau^2\right)\]

Even with missing \(X_i\), observed \(Y_i\) contributes information about model parameters.

Four observation patterns

ObservedMissing\(\int p(y_i\mid x_i,\theta)p(x_i\mid\phi)dx_i\)
MissingMissing\(1\)

Scalar predictor; independent subjects; ignorable missingness; no other observed variables.

Data augmentation

\[\begin{aligned}&p(\theta,\phi,Y_{\mathrm{mis}},X_{\mathrm{mis}}\mid D_{\mathrm{obs}})\\&\qquad\propto p(Y\mid X,\theta)p(X\mid\phi)p(\theta,\phi).\end{aligned}\]

Instead of integrating out missing values, sample them as latent variables.

A sampling cycle

01

Draw missing \(X\) conditional on observed data and current parameters.

02

Optionally draw missing \(Y\) conditional on completed \(X\).

03

Draw \(\theta,\phi\) conditional on completed data.

Repeat. These steps represent a Gibbs-style scheme when the stated full conditionals are available.

The outcome informs missing predictors

\[\boxed{p(x_i\mid y_i,\theta,\phi)\propto p(y_i\mid x_i,\theta)p(x_i\mid\phi)}\]

Imputing \(X\) while ignoring observed \(Y\) can distort regression inference.

Gaussian conditional imputation

\[X_i\mid Y_i=y_i,\theta,\phi\sim N(m_i,v_i)\]
\[v_i=\left(\tau^{-2}+\beta^2/\sigma^2\right)^{-1}\]
\[m_i=v_i\left(\mu_x/\tau^2+\beta(y_i-\alpha)/\sigma^2\right)\]

Two modeling strategies

Joint model

Specify \(p(Y,X\mid\eta)\) directly.

Example: multivariate Gaussian.

Conditional factorization

Specify \(p(Y\mid X,\theta)p(X\mid\phi)\).

Example: logistic regression + Gaussian predictors.

Inference versus prediction

For fully observed new predictors:

\[p(y_*\mid x_*,D)=\int p(y_*\mid x_*,\theta)p(\theta\mid D)\,d\theta\]

For missing new predictors:

\[p(y_*\mid D)=\iiint p(y_*\mid x_*,\theta)p(x_*\mid\phi)p(\theta,\phi\mid D)\,dx_*d\theta d\phi\]

What Bayesian modeling does not solve

  • MAR cannot generally be verified from observed data alone.
  • MNAR mechanisms can be nonidentified.
  • Priors do not create information absent from the likelihood.
  • Misspecifying \(p(X\mid\phi)\) can affect inference about \(\theta\).

Takeaway

When only outcomes are missing, a conditional regression model may suffice.

When predictors are missing, the distribution of predictors becomes part of the inferential problem.

\[p(Y,X)=p(Y\mid X)p(X)\]
Discussion

What is the inferential cost of treating \(X\) as fixed?

Especially when some of its values are unobserved.
